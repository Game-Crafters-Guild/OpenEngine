title: Parallax materials
description: How a height map on a Standard PBR material carves relief below the surface, what the engine decides per pixel, the self-shadow and depth it writes, its cost, its limits, and the steps view.
---

A height map on a Standard PBR material makes a flat surface read as carved: bricks stand proud of their mortar, cobbles of the gaps between them, roof tiles of the row below, and the relief shadows itself away from the sun, most visibly under a low one. The mesh does not change. The relief is carved *below* the polygon, so collision, navigation, and everything placed on the surface stay exactly where they were.

## Setting it up

| Row | What to put there |
|---|---|
| **Height** | A grayscale height map: white is the polygon surface, black the deepest point. Give it the same tiling as the material's other maps. It is imported as one linear channel, whatever its file format or color tag. |
| **Relief Depth** | Appears once a height map is bound. How deep the relief is, as a fraction of one texture repeat: 0.02 on a 2 m repeat is 4 cm. The default is 0.02, the most is 0.04 (see Limits), and 0 turns parallax off without unbinding the map. While the Height row reports a problem (below), the row is inactive: the depth does nothing until the problem is fixed. |

Because the depth is a fraction of a repeat, a texture tiled twice as densely gets a relief half as deep, which keeps its shape: its bricks are half as wide. There is nothing else to set. The engine chooses how many samples each pixel takes, whether the relief shadows itself, and the texture detail it reads, from what the pixel shows (below).

:::caution[When the Height row says no]

A message under the Height row means the material renders without parallax, and says what to change:

- *Parallax cannot combine with hex tiling: turn Hex Tiling off, or remove the Height map*
- *`<surface>` does not use a Height map; bind it on a Standard PBR material*: the Height row is offered only on surfaces that read a height map. A map carried over from another surface lands in the list of other textures, with this message.

:::

## What the engine decides

### How many samples

Each pixel works out how far, in pixels, the full relief depth would shift what it sees. Under half a pixel the relief shifts nothing the pixel can show, so it skips the march and shades the surface where it lies. From half a pixel to one and a half the depth fades in, so there is no line where it starts. Beyond that the pixel takes one step per pixel of shift, up to 32, and then refines the step where the ray entered the relief. A distant surface therefore costs almost nothing, a surface seen head on pays only for its self-shadow, and a grazing close-up gets the full march.

### Self-shadow

The relief shadows itself toward the main directional light, the sun by day and the moon at night: mortar joints and the gaps between stones darken on the side away from the light. Whether a pixel shows it depends on the shadow, not on the view: a wall seen head on shifts nothing on screen but still shows the shadow its bricks cast across the joints below them. The shadow appears once the one the full depth casts is a pixel long on screen and is at full strength by three.

The shadow's edge is softened: relief that only just rises into the light's path lets part of the light through. The softening is a fixed share of each shadow's length, its last fifth or so behind the edge of the relief that casts it, so on screen it grows with the shadow: a shadow a few pixels long still ends in about one pixel, and a long one close to the camera ends in a few pixels of partial shade. Within about a meter of a wall the eight-sample march shows in that edge as a short lighter strip. Under a low sun the shadows stay close to hard, often because a shadow reaches the far side of its joint before its softened tip does. It is wider than a real sun's penumbra, chosen for the look and for a steadier image in motion, and it lies inside the shadow, so the thinnest shadows are a little lighter for it. The same occluder shadows a point the same way whether that point lies deep in a joint or near the top of the relief.

The shadow reads the height map at a level of detail halfway between two. The finer is the detail a pixel resolves along the shadow's own direction, at which a stone gap a pixel wide across that direction keeps its whole shadow. The coarser is the detail across the whole pixel, at which a groove too fine to see rarely darkens a pixel on its own. Halfway is a trade between the two: gaps a few meters away keep most of their shadow, and a surface whose grooves are narrower than a pixel can keep some isolated dark pixels.

How much of a surface the shadow darkens depends on the relief: a deep, narrow mortar joint shows a clear shadow under the default sun, while rounded cobbles at the default depth show little until the sun is low. Point, spot, and area lights light the relief but are not shadowed by it.

### Depth

Where the relief shifts the surface, the depth buffer holds the depth of the relief itself, not the flat polygon: a crate sunk into cobbles shows between the stones down to where the gaps carve below it, and its edge follows the stones instead of cutting a straight line across them. Temporal anti-aliasing reprojects the previous frame through that depth. Shadows stay on the polygon: the surface casts and receives the sun's shadow there, so the part of a sunk prop below the polygon sits in the polygon's shadow, a thin dark band along its base where it meets the relief. Nothing needs setting.

The relief is carved below the polygon, so other geometry that meets the polygon at its edge stands above the carved surface there: a wall whose top is level with a roof's eave shows a thin line of its top along the eave. Keep the top of whatever a parallax surface rests on at least the relief depth below the surface's polygon where the two meet. A roof that sits on its walls rather than flush with their tops does so already.

### Browsers and other WebGPU-class devices

These take a fixed, cheaper version: at most 12 steps, no refinement, no self-shadow, and the depth buffer keeps the flat polygon. Nothing needs setting. The same material works on both.

## Cost

Parallax costs GPU time only where the relief marches or casts its self-shadow. On a current desktop GPU (a release build on an RTX 5080 at 3067 × 900 with 4× MSAA), a view of height-mapped cobbles, bricks, and roof tiles costs 0.01 to 0.07 ms more per frame seen from high above (0.014 ms from 53 m, 0.035 to 0.066 ms from 19 m), where most of the relief is under half a pixel, and about 0.1 ms at eye height beside a brick wall, whether the wall is seen at a slant or head on. A low sun, whose longer shadows the self-shadow follows further, keeps it near 0.1 ms. The browser arm, run on the same GPU, costs a little less. Materials without a height map cost what they did before.

Writing the relief's depth (see Depth) adds the march to the depth prepass. The pass that draws the world then reads that depth instead of marching again. On the same GPU, with a cobbled floor filling the view from eye height beside a sunk crate, that adds about 0.15 ms per frame.

The cost follows the pixels that march or cast a self-shadow and how far their relief shifts, so it grows with the resolution and with how much of the screen a close parallax surface covers. The steps view (below) shows where that is.

## Limits

- **Silhouettes stay straight.** The relief changes what a surface shows, not its outline: the edge of a wall is the edge of its polygon.
- **Shadows meet the polygon, not the relief.** The sun's shadow of a crate falls on the flat surface and the relief casts its outline from the polygon. The relief's own shadowing is the self-shadow above.
- **Cut-outs** (Mask alpha) are decided at the undisplaced surface.
- **Animated meshes** march with their bind-pose tangents.
- **Relief keeps a few dark dots wherever a groove is narrower than a pixel.** At the default depth that is a roof across a street, the far cobbles at a game camera, or an authored brick wall seen head on a few meters away: a scattering of isolated dark pixels where the self-shadow falls into a groove narrower than a pixel (see Self-shadow above). They do not travel with the surface: as the camera moves they come and go from frame to frame, in lines along a roof's courses, a twinkle that MSAA does not smooth and supersampling removes.
- **Relief Depth stops at 0.04.** The self-shadow follows its path in at most eight samples, and a deeper relief casts a longer path. At 0.04 of a repeat a distant surface already shows scattered dark dots where 0.02 shows none. Deeper, the dots multiply and a long shadow's edge breaks into steps near the camera. The row therefore goes no deeper. A later version raises the cap (#2271). The cap is the inspector's: a material file that carries a larger value (an import, a hand edit) renders as written until the row is touched.
- **MSAA does not smooth the self-shadow.** The shadow is worked out once per pixel, so under MSAA without TAA its edges shimmer while the camera moves. The softened edge removes most of the strong pops at a hard edge but keeps a faint crawl of a level or two. Supersampling removes both. TAA holds the edges steady.

## Seeing what the march does

The **Parallax steps** view shows, in the Scene View, how many height samples each pixel of a height-mapped material took, the relief march's and the self-shadow's together, as a color at the brightness of the lit surface. Turn it on with the debug command `set_parallax_steps_view` (`node mcp/ge.mjs set_parallax_steps_view --enable`).

| Color | Samples | Typical of |
|---|---|---|
| gray | none | relief and shadow both under a pixel on screen: distant surfaces |
| blue | 1 to 4 | a self-shadow alone (a wall seen head on), or the shortest march |
| cyan | 5 to 8 | a short march, or a self-shadow with its full path |
| green | 9 to 12 | a surface a few meters away at a moderate angle |
| yellow | 13 to 16 | the most the browser arm takes |
| orange | 17 to 24 | close surfaces seen at a slant |
| red | 25 to 53 | close, grazing surfaces: 53 is the most a desktop pixel takes |

The colors keep their brightness under the view's exposure, but the sky's aerial perspective tints the far distance blue over them, so a distant band can read as blue that is really gray. To read bands exactly, capture the view's scene color target with `capture_resource` on a scene without a sky and take the palette color nearest each pixel's red, green, and blue divided by their sum.
